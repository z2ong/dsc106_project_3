Design Rationale & Development Process

Our team's focus question was, how does projected warming differ across selected regions under lower- and higher-emissions scenarios? Our main motivation behind developing a visualization for this question, was to highlight clearly the historical climate data and climate prediction data trends, filtering regionally in a manner that was easy to follow. We observed that climate change isn't just a universal increase in temperature, but is a non-uniform difference in regional climate. If you skim through our visualization you will see that the Artic has a significant difference in predicted temperature anomaly comparatively to other regions regardless of low or high emission scenarios.

Instead of making the user select regions through a simple drop down, we wanted to make it more interactive by adding map for users to select regions for individual filtering. Users can click on the designated boxes meant to represent each region making the analysis of climate data more engaging. Just below the map, you will see the highlighted temperature anomaly line graph (filterable) highlighting the difference of that region's average temperature from the baseline average we calculated from the historical 1850-1900 climate data. We used line charts to more clearly show the continuous trend and the future difference of in predicted scenarios based on levels of emission. For the color palette, we decided to use a lightish monotone blue for historical data, a darker orange for ssp126 data, and a magenta for ssp585 data to highlight the differences in each scenario. We figured that since blue and orange are on opposing side of the color wheel they would make good contrast. On top of that magenta was between them so our color palette was both visually appealing, but also differing enough to combat color blindess, at least specifically the most common one, red-green blindness.

We sourced our data from the CMIP6 dataset aggregating and filtering for the specific historical and projection data, including longitudinal, temperature, and latitudinal data. We formed our visualization by first thinking of how to allow users to select the regions they wanted to see. We then highlighed the differences through line charts for simple viewing. To conclude our spoon feeding of information, we implemented a table to compare specific pockets of years worth of simulated averages of regional temperature data in case the line graphs weren't specific enough, despite the use of a tooltip to show the year's temperature anomaly data.
